2. Follow the repair from v6 to v11

The two reconstructions in the movie come from the project’s archived EP16 experiment. Its solver used an earlier assumption that both hands were grasping. The labels above were made afterward from the original footage. They show where a future repair should use less guidance.

  1. Fix what the hand should follow. Start from v6. Keep the camera, body size, body root and the block’s position and rotation fixed in each frame. The block still turns exactly as it did in v6.
  2. Adjust the grip. The solver could change the shoulder, elbow, wrist and fingers. It penalized hand points inside the block, drew nearby hand points toward its surface during the assumed grasp, and kept successive adjustments smooth. Rough image marks on the visible right wrist and elbow helped place the arm.
  3. Render and inspect. Show the original and adjusted poses from the same camera. Pause at frames 115 and 130. The near hand sits closer to the block, but the far fingers are hidden in the camera image. Use the hand mesh to check whether those fingers have folded through each other.

For the next run, use the visible right-hand hold to guide contact. Give the uncertain left hand no pull toward the block. Allow a small correction there only when the geometry supports it, and check for intersections within each hand. Keep the starting pose available if the candidate fails.

Training · Contact recognition

Build a reader that returns the right-hand positive label and masks the left-hand unknown. Feed short video crops around each frame to a small model that predicts touch. Keep the right-hand hold together when splitting data.

Deliver a per-hand prediction file with frame ID, state and confidence. Use EP16 to test the pipeline. Learning release, near misses and confidence thresholds requires independently labeled events and separate validation objects.

Training · Pose repair and model lead

Use reviewed, plausible poses as training targets. Add a small known joint error and train the repair to undo it. Include good poses with zero change. Skip v11’s failed left-hand poses as training targets.

Deliver a new same-camera candidate and the same checks. Compare a rule-based repair and the trained repair using identical contact predictions. Approve a version only after it preserves the visible grip and passes the geometry checks.

How the two trainers use this handoff
  1. Train recognition. Keep the existing image encoder fixed. Show about half a second around each labeled frame, with both the hand and target in the crop. Teach the model “touch” on the visible right hand and skip the hidden left-hand answer. This window contributes one hold. Collect distinct clear, touching and release events before fitting a classifier. Keep all footage of the same object in one data group.
  2. Train repair. From independently reviewed training poses, save pairs of an unaltered grip and a version with a small added wrist or finger error. Train the model to recover the reviewed pose using the starting pose, the nearby object surface and the predicted touch confidence. Keep adjustments within joint limits. Include unchanged poses and measure performance on naturally occurring errors.
  3. Isolate each contribution. Give the same rule-based repair contact from distance alone, then from reviewed labels, then from model predictions. With those predictions fixed, compare rule-based and learned pose repair. Choose confidence and movement limits on validation objects. Record acceptable changes in depth, image alignment and timing before the final test.
